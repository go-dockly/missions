There is an enormous amount of material here. WWII is particularly well suited to this concept because the real war was full of clandestine operations, resistance networks, sabotage missions, extraordinary escapes and people whose stories deserve to be told.

I'd actually approach the campaign as a collection of interconnected historical chapters, with our fictional squad and resistance network operating around real events.

One important creative principle: the historical events should remain historically recognisable. Our characters participate in the wider story, rather than secretly taking credit for everything history remembers.

That also gives us freedom to develop our own characters, including their friendships, mistakes, sacrifices and deaths.
1. My strongest candidates
A. Operation Anthropoid — Prague, Czechoslovakia, 1942
Exceptional storytelling potential

27 May – 18 June 1942

Two Czechoslovak parachutists, Jozef Gabčík and Jan Kubiš, attacked Reinhard Heydrich in Prague. Heydrich died on 4 June. The assassins and other resistance fighters eventually took refuge in the crypt of the Church of Saints Cyril and Methodius, where German forces attacked them on 18 June. 

Holocaust Encyclopedia+2

The consequences were horrific. The Nazis destroyed Lidice and Ležáky, murdering inhabitants and deporting others in reprisals against the Czech population. 

Holocaust Encyclopedia+1

Our mission interpretation:

I'd make this a multi-mission Prague chapter.
- Establish safe houses for resistance operatives.
- Transport forged documents.
- Help move underground communications equipment.
- Identify informants and compromised resistance contacts.
- Protect the network after the assassination.
- Conduct an evacuation operation as the Gestapo closes in.
The crypt siege could be a dramatic historical sequence, not necessarily a mission our squad wins.

Narrative opportunity: This is where the squad discovers that even a successful operation can unleash consequences far beyond its original objective.

I'd give this chapter considerable emotional weight. It should be handled with historical sensitivity, especially the fate of civilians.

Ten Royal Marines embarked on a covert mission using folding canoes to attack German merchant shipping in Bordeaux.

They travelled approximately 145 kilometres up the Gironde estuary, operating at night, and placed limpet mines on enemy vessels. The mission was extraordinarily dangerous; several raiders were captured and executed.

This could be one of the most mechanically interesting levels in the game.

Narrative opportunity: A mission where the team knows from the beginning that the odds of everyone returning are extremely poor.
C. Operation Chariot — Saint-Nazaire, France, 1942
Spectacular set-piece





**Best matches for “Gorgopotamos-style” (dramatic natural terrain + real WWII special-ops/sabotage history + usable modern visual coverage) that could plausibly work as a Commandos-style level out of the box:**

### 1. Vemork / Rjukan (Telemark, Norway) – Operation Gunnerside (1943)
The strongest single analogue.  
Norwegian commandos (Kompani Linge) crossed a deep frozen gorge, infiltrated the heavy-water plant perched on a cliff ledge above the valley, and sabotaged the German nuclear program’s key facility without firing a shot. The landscape is pure Commandos: steep gorge, industrial plant clinging to the rock, river/valley below, high plateau approaches, winter conditions, railway elements.

Modern coverage is solid for a remote industrial-heritage site:
- Multiple drone flights over Vemork + Rjukan valley + Gaustatoppen (good aerials of the plant, gorge, and surrounding mountains).
- Tourist/museum walkthroughs of the heavy-water cellar and plant exterior.
- Enough varied viewpoints (aerial, ground, different seasons) that a collector pipeline like the Gorgopotamos one could produce a usable dataset, though not as dense as a major tourist bridge.

Looks and feels the part immediately — industrial + dramatic gorge = classic sabotage level.

### 2. Pointe du Hoc (Normandy, France) – US Rangers, D-Day 1944
100-ft cliffs, bomb-cratered plateau, German casemates and gun positions, Channel views. Rangers scaled the cliffs under fire to neutralize the battery. Extremely “looks the part” for a vertical assault / cliff-climbing / bunker-clearing mission.

YouTube coverage is excellent:
- Many high-quality 4K drone flights showing the cliffs, craters, bunkers, and approach from the sea.
- Walkthroughs, museum context, and repeated filming by Normandy specialists.
- The site is preserved and open, so geometric consistency is high.

Slightly more “D-Day set-piece” than pure small-team sabotage, but the verticality and ruined fortifications make it game-ready.

### 3. Pegasus Bridge / Bénouville (Normandy)
The classic coup-de-main: glider landing right next to the bridge, rapid seizure, then hold against counter-attacks. Canal, bridge structure, nearby château, fields, and the original bridge preserved as a museum piece.

Very high YouTube density (drone flyovers, museum walkarounds, ground-level tours, battlefield walking videos). The site is compact, photogenic, and already heavily documented from multiple angles and seasons. Easy to extract a clean, high-overlap dataset.

### Other notables (weaker on the “out-of-the-box Commandos level” axis)

- **St Nazaire (Operation Chariot, 1942)** — Legendary destroyer-ram raid on the dry dock. Port infrastructure is still there and filmed, but modern commercial activity + larger scale make clean photogrammetry and “small team in dramatic terrain” harder. More of a big-raid set piece.
- **Monte La Difensa / Italian mountains (First Special Service Force)** — Dramatic, but less dense modern tourist drone coverage.
- Various other Normandy battery/bunker sites (Longues-sur-Mer, etc.) — good drone material, but less unique “mission story” punch than the three above.

**Summary ranking for your use case** (dramatic real terrain + authentic special-ops history + enough public video for a frame-extraction pipeline):

1. **Vemork/Rjukan** — closest spiritual and visual twin to Gorgopotamos.  
2. **Pointe du Hoc** — best pure visual “looks like a game level already.”  
3. **Pegasus Bridge** — highest practical coverage and easiest dataset.

Vemork is the one that most feels like “another Gorgopotamos” — remote, gorge-dominated, industrial target, real commando sabotage story. If the collector tool is already tuned for bridge + gorge + mixed aerial/ground, it should transfer reasonably well.

D. Operation Harling — Gorgopotamos, Greece, 1942






















8











25–26 November 1942

A joint operation involving British agents and the Greek resistance organisations EDES and ELAS destroyed a strategically important railway bridge in central Greece. 

Army.gr

What makes this especially interesting is the cooperation between resistance factions that did not necessarily trust one another.

Our mission interpretation:
- Establish contact with two rival resistance groups.
- Negotiate cooperation.
- Reconnoitre the bridge and surrounding mountain passes.
- Secure the approaches.
- Coordinate several teams.
- Carry out the sabotage.
- Manage the withdrawal as German troops respond.
I'd use this chapter to explore political disagreements, distrust and the difficulty of coordinating people who share an enemy but not necessarily an ideology.

Narrative opportunity: The squad learns that getting people to cooperate can be harder than defeating the enemy.

E. The rescue of the Danish Jews — Denmark, 1943
Exceptional human story






















6











September–October 1943

This is perhaps the most important non-military chapter I'd include.

Following the warning that the Nazis planned to deport Denmark's Jewish population, an extensive rescue effort emerged involving ordinary citizens, resistance members, fishermen, police and other helpers.

Approximately 7,200 Danish Jews and 700 non-Jewish relatives escaped to neutral Sweden. 

Holocaust Encyclopedia+1

Our mission interpretation:

The player becomes part of an underground rescue network.
- Find hiding places.
- Arrange forged identity documents.
- Move families through occupied towns.
- Identify informants.
- Coordinate fishing boats.
- Evade German patrols.
- Organise night-time coastal evacuations.
No conventional combat objectives.

The objective is to save as many people as possible.

I'd consider this one of the most emotionally powerful chapters in the game. It would also demonstrate that tactical stealth isn't necessarily about killing anyone.

June 1944 onwards

The Jedburgh teams were small Allied special-operations groups dropped behind enemy lines to support resistance movements.

They coordinated weapons drops, sabotage, intelligence gathering and guerrilla operations in support of the Allied advance. More than 90 teams were deployed. 

CIA+1

Our mission interpretation:

This could be an entire campaign chapter spanning multiple missions.
1. Parachute into occupied France.
2. Locate a resistance contact.
3. Establish radio communication with London.
4. Identify German transport routes.
5. Organise weapons drops.
6. Coordinate attacks against railway infrastructure.
7. Support the advancing Allied forces.
One particularly good feature would be unreliable intelligence.

You might be dropped in the wrong location, lose contact with headquarters and have to find your way to a resistance group without knowing whether they are still operational.

Narrative opportunity: The squad becomes responsible for people who aren't soldiers and cannot simply be ordered around.
H. The Warsaw Uprising — Poland, 1944
Major dramatic campaign chapter






















9











1 August – 2 October 1944

The Polish Home Army launched an uprising intended to liberate Warsaw from German occupation.

It lasted 63 days. The Germans eventually suppressed it and systematically destroyed much of the city. Civilian losses were catastrophic. 

Holocaust Encyclopedia+1

One particularly remarkable historical feature was the use of the sewer network to move fighters and civilians between districts. Thousands escaped through underground passages. 

Muzeum Warszawy

Our mission interpretation:

I'd build a multi-mission urban survival chapter.
- Deliver messages between isolated resistance units.
- Move ammunition and medical supplies.
- Evacuate civilians.
- Protect field hospitals.
- Navigate the sewer network.
- Retrieve wounded operatives.
- Hold evacuation corridors.
This shouldn't become a fantasy in which four commandos win the Warsaw Uprising.

The historical outcome must remain intact.

Instead, the squad's story would focus on the people they manage to protect and the people they cannot save.

It could be devastatingly powerful.

3. How I'd structure our campaign
I wouldn't necessarily use all these events. That could become a history textbook rather than a coherent story.

I'd select around eight major chapters and connect them through the lives of our characters.

Here's a possible structure:
THE SHADOW FRONT
Campaign I — The Resistance
PROPOSED HISTORICAL CAMPAIGN STRUCTURE · 1942–1945
1. The Invasion — France, 1942
   
   Bruneval-inspired intelligence operation. Introduce the squad, its specialists and its command structure.
2. The Black City — Prague, 1942
   
   Operation Anthropoid and the aftermath. Introduce the darker moral dimensions of clandestine warfare.
3. The Iron Bridge — Greece, 1942
   
   Gorgopotamos. Establish resistance alliances and coordinated squad operations.
4. The Silent Harbour — Bordeaux, 1942
   
   Operation Frankton-inspired river infiltration. A smaller specialist operation with enormous tension.
5. White Silence — Norway, 1943
   
   Vemork. The signature industrial infiltration and Luc's devastating story.
6. The Escape — Denmark, 1943
   
   The rescue of Danish Jews. A deeply human chapter that changes the squad's perspective.
7. Behind Enemy Lines — France, 1944
   
   Jedburgh-inspired campaign. The resistance network expands, and the squad operates alongside a wider Allied offensive.
8. The Final Reckoning — Italy, 1945
   
   Operation Tombola-inspired finale. The full resistance network comes together for one final operation.

This is a narrative proposal, not a suggestion that the same historical people or original units participated in all these operations. We'd need to make the movement of our fictional squad between theatres believable.
4. The feature I would build specifically around this historical campaign
I'd introduce a historical intelligence dossier.

Before every chapter, the player receives a briefing containing:








frontlinestories.com


- Historical background.
- Geographic intelligence.
- Known enemy installations.
- Available resistance contacts.
- Primary and secondary objectives.
- Intelligence confidence.
- Known historical figures and organisations.
- Uncertainties and potential complications.
The player should gradually learn that intelligence is never complete.

Some documents are outdated. Informants can be unreliable. Enemy patrol strengths can be misreported. Sometimes the mission briefing is simply wrong.

This ties the historical authenticity directly into our gameplay systems.

One thing I'd be particularly keen on is avoiding a campaign consisting exclusively of sabotage missions. Rescuing civilians, gathering intelligence, protecting resistance members, escaping compromised operations and making difficult moral decisions should all be equally important gameplay experiences.

I think the real opportunity is to make this a WWII story told through the people who fought the secret war, rather than another story about winning the conventional war.

That gives us an enormous historical canvas, genuinely distinctive missions and, most importantly, a reason for players to care deeply about the people they lead.
